# A8 – Gears

## Objective
The objective of this assignment was to design a functional two-gear gearbox that could be assembled and disassembled while maintaining proper shaft alignment, bearing support, and gear clearance. The gearbox uses an 18-tooth pinion and a 48-tooth spur gear, providing a gear ratio of 24:9. The required output torque is 1.25 in-lbf at 1100 rpm. The input and output shafts have diameters of 0.125 in and 0.250 in, respectively, and are each 1 in long.

The assignment involved analyzing shaft strength, designing journal bearings, selecting appropriate materials, and developing a two-piece gearbox housing using SolidWorks. The main goal was to apply mechanical design calculations to a complete assembly while considering how each component interacts with the others.

## Analyze
I began by identifying the known and unknown values for the gearbox and reviewing the relationship between rotational speed, torque, and gear ratio. I then calculated the forces and stresses acting on the input and output shafts to determine whether the specified diameters were sufficient.

Assuming an ideal gear train, I calculated the input torque as 0.469 in-lbf and the input rotational speed as 2933.33 rpm. Using the torsional shear stress equation for a solid circular shaft, I determined that the input shaft experiences approximately 1222 psi, while the output shaft experiences approximately 407 psi.

I then analyzed the journal bearings required to support both rotating shafts. This involved calculating the forces acting on the bearings, bearing pressure, surface velocity, and pressure-velocity (PV) rating. These calculations helped determine which bearing material would provide adequate support without excessive wear or friction.

I also considered the forces generated by the meshing gears and how those forces would be distributed between the bearings. For the initial design, I assumed that the bearings shared the radial load equally and that the gear train operated without power losses.

Finally, I examined the dimensions of the gears, shafts, and bearings to determine the required housing geometry. The housing needed to provide sufficient internal clearance for the gears while maintaining the correct center distance and allowing the components to be installed and removed.

Input Shaft

Known values:

Diameter: 0.125 in

Length: 1 in

Input torque: 0.469 in-lbf

Input speed: 2933.33 rpm

Using the torsional shear stress equation:

τ = 16T / (πd³)

τ = 16(0.46875) / [π(0.125)³]

Maximum shear stress = 1222.31 psi

The input shaft experiences the greatest torsional stress because of its smaller diameter. The calculated stress is relatively low for common engineering shaft materials, making the specified diameter a reasonable starting point.

Output Shaft

Known values:

Diameter: 0.250 in

Length: 1 in

Output torque: 1.25 in-lbf

Output speed: 1100 rpm

Using the same equation:

τ = 16(1.25) / [π(0.250)³]

Maximum shear stress = 407.44 psi

Although the output shaft transmits more torque, its larger diameter significantly reduces the torsional shear stress. Both shaft diameters appear adequate under the assumed torsional loading, subject to confirmation using the selected material's allowable shear stress.

Journal Bearing Analysis

The journal bearings were designed to support the shafts while allowing them to rotate inside the gearbox housing.

The main considerations were bearing pressure, sliding velocity, PV rating, material selection, and wall thickness.

For the bearing analysis, I considered the tangential and radial forces produced by the meshing gears and assumed the load was distributed equally between two supporting bearings on each shaft.

The following relationships were used:

Bearing pressure: P = F / (Ld)

Surface velocity: V = πdn / 12

PV rating: PV = P × V

Where F is the bearing load in lbf, L is the bearing length in inches, d is the shaft diameter in inches, and n is the rotational speed in rpm. Surface velocity is expressed in ft/min.

The calculated pressure and velocity values were used to evaluate suitable bearing materials and determine whether the bearings could operate within their recommended limits.

## Decide
Bearing Material and Selection

I selected oil-impregnated bronze as the journal bearing material because of its wear resistance, self-lubricating properties, and suitability for rotating shaft applications. This material reduces the need for an external lubrication system and is commonly used in compact mechanical assemblies.

I considered McMaster-Carr sleeve bearings for both the input and output shafts, selecting dimensions based on the required shaft diameters and available space inside the gearbox.

The proposed bearing components were:

Input shaft: McMaster-Carr 6391K111

Output shaft: McMaster-Carr 6391K131

These part numbers and their actual dimensional tolerances must be checked against the final CAD model before ordering.

Bearing Fits and Housing Clearance

I used a running sliding fit between the shafts and journal bearings to allow rotation without excessive friction. The bearings are intended to be press-fit into the gearbox housing so they remain stationary during operation.

I also allowed approximately 0.0625 in of clearance around the gears to reduce the risk of interference between rotating components and the housing.

The bearing locations and shaft center distance were important because even small alignment errors could prevent the gears from meshing properly.

Gearbox Housing

I decided to use a two-piece housing consisting of a base and removable cover. This allows the gearbox to be assembled and disassembled without permanently joining the components.

The housing was designed around the gear arrangement rather than forcing the gears into predetermined dimensions. I considered the gear outside diameters, shaft positions, journal bearing locations, and required internal clearances while developing the geometry.

The two halves are intended to be secured using four #2-56 screws, with alignment pins providing additional positioning accuracy.

SolidWorks Model

After completing the initial calculations, I developed the gearbox geometry using parametric CAD modeling.

The design includes:

An 18-tooth input pinion

A 48-tooth output gear

A 0.125 in diameter input shaft

A 0.250 in diameter output shaft

Journal bearings for both shafts

A two-piece gearbox housing

Fastening and alignment features

I created an assembled gearbox model and an exploded configuration to demonstrate how the components fit together.

The initial geometry was generated as SolidWorks-compatible STEP files. These files can be imported into SolidWorks for further editing, assembly refinement, and inspection.

The gear profiles in the preliminary model are approximations, so the final design requires the exact manufacturer CAD geometry to verify gear engagement and motion.

Design Limitations

One limitation of the design is the specified 1 in shaft length. Depending on the housing thickness and bearing placement, the shafts may not extend far enough outside the housing to connect to a motor or an external load.

Another consideration is that the initial shaft analysis primarily evaluates torsional stress. A complete design would also verify bending stresses, shaft deflection, material strength, and an appropriate factor of safety.

Finally, the journal bearing fits must be checked using actual manufacturer tolerances to ensure that the shafts rotate freely while the bearings remain securely positioned in the housing.

## Communicate
Mistakes Made

One of the first challenges was distinguishing the gear ratio from the actual number of teeth on each gear. The specified 24:9 ratio corresponds to the 48-tooth output gear and 18-tooth input pinion. Understanding this relationship was important when calculating the torque, rotational speed, and gear center distance.

Another challenge was determining the correct housing dimensions. Initially, it was easy to focus on the gears themselves without accounting for the additional space required by the shafts, bearings, and fastening features. This reinforced the importance of planning the entire assembly before finalizing individual components.

I also had to consider the difference between fixed and floating components when working with the assembly in SolidWorks. Keeping the base housing fixed while allowing the remaining components to move made it easier to understand how the parts should be positioned and constrained.

The imported STEP geometry also presented a limitation because it did not include the original SolidWorks feature history. This demonstrated the difference between importing existing CAD geometry and creating a fully editable parametric model.

Lessons Learned

This assignment taught me how important it is to consider the relationship between every component in a mechanical assembly.

One of the most significant observations was how strongly shaft diameter affects torsional shear stress. Even though the output shaft transmits more torque, its larger diameter produces a lower calculated stress than the input shaft.

I also learned that journal bearing design involves more than selecting a bearing with the correct inside diameter. The material, bearing length, operating pressure, surface velocity, PV rating, and shaft clearance all influence performance.

Another lesson was understanding the difference between press fits and running sliding fits. The gears need to remain attached to the shafts, while the bearings must allow the shafts to rotate freely. Selecting the incorrect fit could prevent the gearbox from functioning properly.

Designing the housing also reinforced the importance of assembly and maintenance. The position of the housing split, fasteners, and bearings determines how easily the gearbox can be assembled and disassembled.

Finally, I gained a better understanding of how to use imported components and parametric CAD tools together. This assignment demonstrated the importance of checking dimensions, component interference, alignment, and assembly constraints rather than relying only on the visual appearance of a model.

Overall, this project helped connect engineering calculations with practical mechanical design and demonstrated how shafts, bearings, gears, and housing components work together to transmit rotational motion.

I spent approximately 6-7 hours working on this assignment. Most of the time was dedicated to the shaft and bearing calculations, determining the housing dimensions, and developing the gearbox model for SolidWorks.
